# Business Value Decision Tree — Master Logic, Popup Copy & Gap Analysis

## Overview

The Business Value track is designed to help founders discover that their company may have significant intangible assets — brand, IP, software, customer base, contracts — that are not captured by a standard profit or revenue multiple. Most founders do not know these assets exist as a formal valuation category. The assessment's job is to surface that awareness and connect it to a commercial event.

The core commercial framing is a **sum-of-parts valuation**: a standard financial multiple (e.g., 2x EBITDA) captures the operating business, while an intangibles valuation layer adds a separately substantiated component on top. A company valued at 2x on financials alone may be worth 3.5x when brand, IP, and customer relationships are formally documented and included. This is not inflating the number — it is making visible what already exists but is not reflected in the P&L.

## Business Value Track Questions & Popup Copy

---

### Q1_BV. How is your company usually valued today?

| Option | Popup Copy |
|---|---|
| Profit multiple | That is the most common method for SMEs and profitable businesses. It is a useful starting point, but a profit multiple only captures what shows up in your income statement. It typically misses the value of your brand, customer relationships, software, data, IP, and systems — all of which can add meaningful value on top of the financial baseline. |
| Revenue multiple | Revenue multiples are common in growth-stage companies where profitability is secondary to scale. However, like profit multiples, they do not account for the quality of your revenue — whether it is recurring, contract-backed, or driven by a strong brand — or for the intangible assets that make that revenue defensible. |
| Asset value | Asset-based valuation is standard in property, manufacturing, and asset-heavy businesses. It almost always misses intangible value entirely — brand equity, customer loyalty, proprietary systems, and specialist know-how are rarely reflected on a balance sheet. |
| Comparable companies | Benchmarking against comparable companies is a useful sanity check, but it assumes your business is average. If you have stronger customer retention, better IP, or a more defensible market position than your peers, a comparables approach will undervalue you. |
| Investor negotiation | Negotiated valuations can work in your favour — but only if you control the narrative. If you cannot clearly articulate and evidence what makes your business worth more than a basic multiple, investors will use their own assumptions, which are usually conservative. |
| I do not know | That is more common than you might think, and it is actually a useful starting point. Many founders only discover how their business is being valued when they are already in a fundraising or sale conversation. Understanding your current valuation baseline is the first step to improving it. |

### Q3_BV. What do you think your current valuation does not fully capture? (Multi-select)

| Option | Popup Copy |
|---|---|
| Brand or reputation | Brand value is real but often invisible in financial statements. If customers choose you over competitors because of who you are — not just what you charge — that preference has commercial value. It can influence pricing power, customer acquisition costs, and acquisition premiums. |
| Customer relationships | Strong, long-standing customer relationships reduce churn, increase lifetime value, and make the business more defensible. These are often undervalued because they do not appear on a balance sheet, but they are exactly what buyers and investors are paying for. |
| Repeat customers or recurring revenue | This is one of the strongest value signals a business can have. Predictable, recurring revenue reduces investor risk and supports a higher valuation multiple. If a significant portion of your revenue comes back without you having to re-sell, that is a structural advantage worth documenting. |
| Long-term contracts | Contracts provide revenue visibility and reduce risk. They are a concrete, documentable form of intangible value — particularly if they are with well-known clients, span multiple years, or include renewal clauses. |
| Software, platform, app, or internal technology | Proprietary technology creates value when it improves efficiency, supports customers, generates data, or gives the business a competitive advantage that cannot be easily replicated. Even internal tools that reduce headcount or improve margins can be formally valued as an intangible asset. |
| Data or customer database | A well-maintained customer database or proprietary dataset can be a significant asset — particularly if it drives decisions, enables personalisation, or would be difficult for a competitor to replicate. The key is whether it is structured, maintained, and actively used. |
| IP, trademarks, patents, designs, or know-how | Registered IP is one of the most clearly documentable forms of intangible value. Even unregistered know-how — proprietary methods, formulas, or processes — can be valued if it is documented and defensible. |
| Operating systems, processes, or playbooks | Well-documented systems and processes reduce key-person dependency and make the business more scalable and transferable. This is particularly relevant in service businesses where value is often trapped in the founder's head rather than in the organisation. |
| Technical team or specialist knowledge | A team with rare expertise is a competitive advantage — but only if that knowledge is captured in systems, processes, or documentation. If it walks out the door when someone leaves, it is harder to value formally. |
| None of the above | That is useful to know. The remaining questions will help identify whether there are other value signals worth exploring — particularly around your revenue quality, customer relationships, or market position. |
| I am not sure | That is a very common answer. Most founders only realise what value they have built after mapping their assets more carefully. The remaining questions are designed to help you do exactly that. |

### Q5_BV. Why do you want a stronger valuation?

| Option | Popup Copy |
|---|---|
| We are raising funds | Fundraising is one of the clearest reasons to formally document your intangible assets. Institutional investors — including VCs and growth equity funds — do consider intangible assets when they are properly packaged and substantiated. A sum-of-parts valuation that clearly separates your financial multiple from your intangibles layer gives investors a structured, credible basis for understanding why your business is worth more than a basic revenue or profit multiple. |
| We may sell the business | Sellers who understand and can evidence their hidden value are in a significantly stronger negotiating position. Buyers are trained to discount anything they cannot verify. If your brand, customer relationships, IP, or systems are not formally documented, buyers will simply exclude them from the price. |
| We are bringing in investors or shareholders | New investors and shareholders will form their own view of value. If you do not have a clear, documented picture of your intangible assets before they arrive, you are negotiating without your strongest cards on the table. |
| We are planning succession or restructuring | Succession and restructuring often require a credible, independent business valuation. Intangible assets can significantly affect how the business is divided, transferred, or priced — particularly in family businesses or management buyouts. |
| We need to explain our value to the board, bank, auditor, or partners | Explaining value to stakeholders requires evidence, not just claims. A structured view of your intangible assets gives you a clear, defensible narrative that is much easier to communicate than a general assertion that the business is worth more. |
| We want to understand what the business is really worth | That is a strong reason to start. Even without an immediate transaction, knowing where your business value comes from — and what is not yet reflected in your financials — helps you make better decisions about growth, investment, and strategy. |
| No specific reason. Just curious. | Curiosity is a perfectly valid starting point. Many founders only realise the value gap after seeing what parts of the business are not reflected in normal financial numbers. This assessment may surface something worth paying attention to. |

### Q7_BV. What is your biggest concern about your company's valuation?

| Option | Popup Copy |
|---|---|
| Investors may not understand the true value of the business | That is a common and legitimate concern. Investors — particularly institutional ones — are trained to anchor on financial metrics. But when intangible assets are properly packaged and substantiated, they do factor into the conversation. The key is presenting them in a format that investors can evaluate, not just assert. |
| Buyers may value us too cheaply | That is a real risk. Buyers are incentivised to find reasons to discount the price. If your brand, customer relationships, IP, or systems are not formally documented and evidenced, buyers will simply exclude them from their offer. |
| Our brand, software, data, IP, or customer base is not reflected properly | That is exactly the gap a formal intangibles valuation is designed to address. These assets may not show clearly in the financials, but they can still influence what the business is worth — particularly to a strategic buyer or growth investor. |
| We do not know how to prove the business is worth more | That is often the main gap. The business may be genuinely valuable, but the value needs to be organised into evidence that outsiders can understand and accept. This is a process problem, not a value problem. |
| We are being valued only on profit or revenue | That can be frustrating if your business has more going on beneath the surface. A basic multiple may miss the quality, stickiness, systems, and assets behind the numbers — all of which can support a meaningfully higher valuation when formally documented. |
| We are not concerned. Just exploring. | That is a useful starting point. Even if there is no immediate concern, this assessment can help you understand where business value may sit outside the usual financial numbers — and what you might want to start documenting before a transaction eventually happens. |

## Outcome Logic: Lead Temperature Classification

### Definitions

**Strong Event Trigger:** Q5_BV = Fundraising, Selling, or Bringing in investors.
**Strong Asset Signal:** Q3_BV includes Recurring revenue, Contracts, Software, or IP — or at least 3 other assets selected.
**Near-Term Urgency:** Q6_BV = Now or Within 3 months.
**Strong Pain:** Q2_BV = "Yes, definitely" OR Q7_BV = Investors don't understand, Buyers value too cheaply, or Assets not reflected.

### Lead Rules

| Condition | Lead |
|---|---|
| Strong Event Trigger + Near-Term Urgency + (Strong Pain OR Strong Asset Signal) | **HOT** |
| Strong Event Trigger + Far-Term Urgency + (Strong Pain OR Strong Asset Signal) | **WARM** |
| No Strong Event Trigger + Strong Asset Signal + Strong Pain | **WARM** |
| Strong Event Trigger + Near-Term Urgency, but no Strong Pain and no Strong Asset Signal | **WARM** |
| No Strong Event Trigger + No Near-Term Urgency + Weak/No Asset Signal | **COLD** |
| Q5_BV = Just curious + Q6_BV = No timeline | **COLD** |
